“The orbit of a planet is an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.”Eccentricity e = c / a tells how stretched the ellipse is: 0 is a circle, close to 1 is very stretched.

Try this

  1. Turn on Foci, then String. Watch d₁ + d₂ while the planet moves. Does the total change? This is your pins-and-string ellipse lab.
  2. Drag the velocity arrow until the two foci meet. What is e now, and what shape is the orbit?
  3. Turn on Eccentricity. Pick Earth, then Mercury, as the Target Orbit and press “Put my planet on this orbit”. Which is more stretched? Compare with the slides (e = 0.1 and e = 0.7).
  4. Turn on Axes. Is the star at the centre of the ellipse?

Speed detective

(T₁ / T₂)² = (r₁ / r₂)³The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the average distance (the semi-major axis, a). For our Sun, T² / a³ = 1 when T is in years and a is in AU.

Find the pattern

  1. Drag the planet to make three or four different orbits. Each new orbit leaves a point on the graph.
  2. Try the T and a buttons. Which pair gives a straight line through the origin?
  3. Turn on Period (T) and Semi-major Axis (a). Check that T² ÷ a³ stays the same for every orbit.
  4. Move the Star Mass slider. Does T² ÷ a³ stay the same? What does that tell you?

The look and controls follow the layout of PhET Interactive Simulations' Kepler's Laws (University of Colorado Boulder, phet.colorado.edu); this is a separate lesson tool built for this class.
